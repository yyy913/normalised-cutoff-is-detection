Reviewers' Comments: 
Reviewer #1: 
Remarks to the Author: 
In this manuscript titled as ‘Unraveling lncRNA Diversity at a Single Cell Resolution and in a Spatial Context across Different
Cancer Types’, the authors have analyzed single-cell and spatial transcriptomics data to uncover millions of previously not-
reported lncRNAs in 13 cancer types. They also analyzed these lncRNAs using long read sequencing data. By establishing
the co-expressions between these lncRNAs and cancer-associated protein coding genes, the authors claimed the functional
correlation of these lncRNAs in cancer. In addition, previously reported disease-associated genetic variants were found within
these lncRNAs. Based on these findings, these identified lncRNAs were given a group name as uTAR or cuTAR, and a
website ‘SPanC-Lnc’ has been built on AWS cloud. Overall, this is a descriptive work. The detailed features and functions of
these lncRNAs await for being clarified. 
Major points: 
1.‘uTAR’ stands for ‘unannotated Transcriptionally Active Regions’. This name should be changed, because ‘unannotated’ is
not appropriate. These uTARs, as a manner of fact, have been ‘annotated’ by the authors already if these lncRNAs will be
experimentally confirmed by further investigations. 
2.The name of ‘uTAR/cuTAR’ is only used by the authors so far. To make their website more friendly to other users, it would be
much helpful to use genomic location or other suitable searching ID, instead of cuTAR ID, for ‘visualization’ when these users
don’t know whether there is a cuTAR or not yet on these users' interested genomic regions. 
3.To confirm the existence of the uTARs/cuTARs, the authors must perform northern and primer extension experiments to
obtain the information of these uTARsuTARs/cuTARs’ start sites, end points and exon/intron junctions. And use their datasets
to analysis the potential splicing isoforms of the uTARs/cuTARs. 
4.The following information in detail should be provided in the manuscript: quality of single-cell full-length transcriptome data,
RNA-sequence length distribution, sequence accuracy and completeness, PolyA tail length, and unique molecular identifiers
(UMIs). 
5.It is actually a little bit surprising to find such a huge number of new lncRNAs after the recent years of studies on single-cell
and spatial transcriptomics data. Please provide the alignment ratio to the reference genome on known protein coding genes’
exons. 
Minor points: 
Overall, the language and figure format need to be polished. 
Language: 
First, many sentences are too complicated and difficulty to follow. For instance, Fig.4e legend ‘e, Co-expression of
cuTAR215705 with the mRNA encoding the RNA-binding protein ELAVL1 and its interacting partner BIRC5 and the mRNA
encoding TARDBP5 in breast cancer.’ (quoted) Could it be simplified as ‘e, Co-expression of cuTAR215705 with BIRC5,
ELAVL1 and TARDBP5 in breast cancer.’? Similar issues are found at many other places. Please make sure to check the
entire manuscript. 
Second, the authors need to go through the manuscript to avoid mistakes or typos. Example: line536 ‘(HPV’ (quoted). Similar
issues are found at many other places. Please make sure to check the entire manuscript. 
Third, the punctuation should be correctly used. For example, line411 ‘Specifically, from OPSCC (HNC) we obtained...’
(quoted). lines784-788 ‘This study not only provides coordinates of the identified transcripts but also provides a comparison of
expression across different cancer types and types and analyzes their putative functions.’(quoted). For these two sentences, at
least a comma should be added in front of 'we obtained' and ‘but also’. Similar issues are found at many other places. Please
make sure to check the entire manuscript. 
Figure format: 
Enlarge the text size, unify the figure format. 
Reviewer #2: 
Remarks to the Author: 
The authors have generated multiple spatial transcriptomics datasets, combined them with scRNA analysis of lncRNAs,
particularly novel and unannotated lncRNAs, and investigated their overlap with regulatory features and disease-associated
genetic variants. They also examined co-expressed cancer-associated protein-coding genes and strengthened their results
through experimental validation, constructing a spatially resolved lncRNA atlas across different cancer and tissue types. While
I have the following questions: 
1. In Fig. S3b, why is KCNQ1OT highly expressed in breast cancer samples, and why is TINCR highly expressed in SCC and
HNC without showing corresponding cancer specificity? 
2. In Fig. S4, lncRNA statistics from FANTOM, LncExpDB, and NONCODE are presented, with NONCODE showing some
lncRNAs not included in LncExpDB and FANTOM. Why were novel uTARs in Fig. 1c not filtered against NONCODE data? 
3. The CPAT function for calculating coding potential scores is significantly affected by transcript length. Since the 3' ends of
the library do not represent full-length transcripts, using CPAT scoring may result in lower scores. The authors are encouraged
to compare their results with other scoring tools to determine whether there is overlap or discrepancy in the identified high
translational potential lncRNAs. 
4. In Fig. 3b, cuTAR121087 shows higher expression in the tumor region compared to the normal region. However, in H&N
cancer samples from TCGA, this lncRNA is reported differently. What accounts for this discrepancy? 
5. Line 414 mentions that 31.7% of 3' ends of fragmented transcripts captured by 10X Visium were validated by ONT. Has the
author analyzed why 68.3% were not detected? Are these non-detected lncRNAs different from the validated 31.7%, and why
is such a large portion of uTARs not detected? It seems a little strange. 
6. Line 506 mentions the top eight uTARs in Fig. 4a, but the figure shows only three. 
7. In Case Study 1, line 693, the sample of Acral Melanoma was collected from a poor responder, with cuTAR94762
downregulated post-treatment, co-localized with an eQTL SNP associated with RASGRF1. Why does Fig. 6a show higher
expression post-treatment? 
8. The supplementary figures lack detailed annotations, which hinders understanding. Many figures also have inconsistent text
sizes and unclear legends, such as in Fig. S6. 
9. It is mentioned in methods that the genome is split into non-overlapping 50bp bins, labeled as transcriptionally active if at
least three reads are detected in that bin. If a read just overlaps with a 50 bp bin, will it still be counted? 
Reviewer #3: 
Remarks to the Author: 
Emerging evidence indicates that long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) play regulatory roles in gene expression regulation, and
their dysregulation is associated with diseases such as cancers. However, owning to their low abundance, cell-type and
tissue-type specific expression pattern, and the limitation of unbiased detection approaches, the spatial distribution and
pathophysiological functions of lncRNAs are far from clear. 
In this manuscript, the authors have integrated 36 spatial-resolved RNA sequencing and single-cell sequencing data from 13
different cancers, and uncovered the spatial distribution information of over 350 thousand lncRNAs at the single-cell level.
Importantly, they have deposited the annotation and expression data of lncRNAs (termed ‘SPanC-Lnc’) on the AWS cloud,
which represents the largest resource of pan-cancer annotations of lncRNAs with single-cell and spatial context. By combining
this information with available toolkits, the authors have identified the co-expression of tumor-specific lncRNAs with cancer-
associated genes as well as their co-localization patterns with predicted interacting proteins. In addition, they have also
suggested specific cases to indicate the potential application of the lncRNA spatial distribution information in analyzing the
responses of cancer therapy and serving as prognostic markers. 
Overall, this manuscript has provided a useful recourse of the lncRNA spatial distribution information at the single-cell
resolution, which is helpful to understand the distribution features and functional potential of lncRNAs in the context of cancers.
However, this manuscript unfortunately does not provide any novel or advanced methods for the lncRNA studies, rather is a
combination of existing single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomic sequencing, and other existing methods as a dataset. It is
more appropriate for a journal specialized in data resources at the current stage. 
Other major concerns: 
1) Considering the low abundance of most lncRNAs at the cellular level, it is critical to evaluate the reliability of signals from
single-cell and special transcriptomic sequencing. How many of the signals detected are false positive? How is the signals
overlapping with bulk RNA sequencing? How much input samples are subjected for sequencing? In Supplemental Figure 5c,
the overlap of different sequencing data is fairly low, does it indicate most of the signals are false positive? 
2) In Figure 1a, only one sample for each type of cancer was analyzed by scRNA-seq, which is insufficient and can be biased.
To conclude the pan-cancer information of lncRNAs, at least triplicate samples are needed to provide a convincing dataset. 
3) In Figure 2c, how does the authors define lncRNAs conservation? Is it solely based on the primary sequence similarity?
Alternatively, are the structure conservation or positional conservation taken into consideration? It is confusing to see the
conservation score of MALAT1 and HOTAIR is higher than the coding RNA GAPDH and RPL5. 
4) In Figures 4b and 4e, and Figure 5, it is necessary to experimentally detect the co-expression and co-localization of
lncRNAs with corresponding cancer-associated genes and interacting proteins. 
5) For lncRNAs that were suggested to indicate responses to cancer therapy and as biomarkers in Figure 6, how many of them
are found from previous bulk RNA sequencing? What is their abundance in general and individually? Again, it is critical to
evaluate whether their expression is false positive. 
Minor concerns: 
1) The logical of the manuscript needs to polish. There are some confusions in the figures and text: 
- Line 231, “157 uTARs were expressed on a pan-cancer level”; however, as shown in Supplemental Figure 5, 210 uTARs
were labeled. 
- Line 235, “Supplemental Fig. 4b” appeared to be “Supplemental Fig. 5b” 
- Line 262, Figure 2a, it is unable to see 95.7% of the uTARs lack the coding potential. 
- Lines 640 and 641, Figure 5c, PMEL and MLANA are not in proximal. 
2) Other mistakes: i.e. Line 477 double “we”; Line 652 LINC00589 is mislabeled to LINC0058.

Reviewer #1: 
Remarks to the Author: 
In this manuscript titled as ‘Unraveling lncRNA Diversity at a Single Cell Resolution and in a 
Spatial Context across Different Cancer Types’, the authors have analyzed single-cell and spatial 
transcriptomics data to uncover millions of previously not-reported lncRNAs in 13 cancer types. 
They also analyzed these lncRNAs using long read sequencing data. By establishing the co -
expressions between these lncRNAs and cancer-associated protein coding genes, the authors 
claimed the functional correlation of these lncRNAs in cancer. In addition, previously reported 
disease-associated genetic variants were found within these lncRNAs. Based on these findings, 
these identified lncRNAs were given a group name as uTAR or cuTAR, and a website ‘SPanC-
Lnc’ has been built on AWS cloud. Overall, this is a descriptive work. The detailed features and 
functions of these lncRNAs await for being clarified. 
 
We thank the reviewer for the comment. This manuscript, submitted to ‘Nature Methods Resource’ 
aims to provide the first and most comprehensive spatial and single cell lncRNA database, 
especially focusing on unannotated or uncharacterized lncRNAs. This first data resource will 
provide the community with a reference of expression abundance and spatial location, visually 
searchable and mappable using our AWS interactive cloud application.  
 
Our method and resource enables novel analysis of the potential function of any cuTAR through 
colocalization with known coding genes and functional annotation. By providing the identity of 
specific cell types and sub-tumor regions where existing lncRNAs are expressed, this approach 
introduces a new feature that can indicate potential functional roles. Our analyses show that a 
subset of these cuTARs (the cancer associated unannotated Transcriptionally Active Regions that 
are likely lncRNAs)  are coexpressed with known markers and/or causal genes, co-localize with 
known causal SNPs, are novel markers of cell clusters/cell types, and are predictive features for 
survival or drug responses.  
 
This high-throughput spatial analysis allows researchers to confidently prioritize lncRNAs for 
targeted validation experiments, tailored to specific diseases and anticipated outcomes, such as 
overall biological function or drug target potential. These insights were previously unattainable 
 
2 
without spatial and/or single-cell resolution, highlighting the unique opportunities our method 
provides for functional exploration of lncRNAs in tumor tissue. 
 
As specific examples of how to leverage this valuable data resource for understanding/discovering 
lncRNA functions, we showed that our spatial, functional analysis revealed the likely roles of 
cuTARs cuTAR288960 and cuTAR94762 in response to anti-PD1 therapy in Melanoma (Figure 
5) and the expression of cuTAR215705 in stratifying patient survival in breast cancers (better than

A uTAR-to-gene alignment ratio is greatly influenced by the coverage of the individual samples.

Only up to about 1.6% of the aligned reads map to uTARs. The most likely reasons for the low 
genome mapping coverage are that:  1) Visium only measures up to 200 bp from the 3’ end, and

protein-coding RNA, and 3) the cell type specific nature of uTAR compared to that of the coding 
genes. Nevertheless, our stringent pipeline detected 90,533 cuTARs consistently in at least two 
cancer types, and ~58% of all cuTARs overlap with FANTOM/LncBook/NONCODE lncRNAs.

at least 10 cancer types.  
 
Among the 219,442 uTARs we reported, the majority of them are unique to one cancer type 
(Manuscript Supplementary Fig. 5a,b) and are expressed at low levels, likely be filtered during 
downstream analyses in most projects, which only considered one cell type and/or only abundant 
genes. Overall, ~15,000 uTARs were detected per sample, depending on the coverage. These 
numbers are comparable to those from other studies. LncExpDB reports 101,293 human lncRNAs, 
and the FANTOM-CAGE study reports 27,919 lncRNAs. However, not all these lncRNAs are 
expressed in all individuals and are also cancer-specific. There were a few studies that have 
profiled lncRNAs in skin cancers using different methods. For example, a study by Wang et al.2  
profiled lncRNAs in immune cells of PBMCs from patients with Melanoma, identifying 24,881

RNA-sequencing. A comprehensive study of Iyer et al. 3 identified more than 58,000 mature 
lncRNAs from 7256 RNA libraries derived from human tissues, including 5298 primary cancers 
and 281 metastases. Given the higher throughput, enabling profiling thousands of cells and 
measuring expression at single-cell level, the high numbers detected are expected.

76 cuTARs were chosen for validation with targeted probes using single cell spatial 
transcriptomics Xenium platform to verify their cancer-type specificity as observed using 10X 
Visium. Five genomic DNA regions around centromeres that are unlikely to transcribe into RNA 
were chosen as a negative control set to distinguish whether the detected cuTAR signals are not 
noise from genomic DNA, but true RNA transcripts. The sequences for probe designing were 
selected based on performing multiple sequence alignment using ClustalW across the in-house 
Visium, spatial long read and scRNA samples, and extracting the most represented sequences. The 
sequences in fasta format were sent to the 10X Bioinformatics team, who applied their advanced 
pipeline to design probes for target domains with stringent tests for off-targets (Probe design 
provided in Supplementary Table S3).

We generated more data from Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks of two 
Head & Neck OPSCC, three melanoma, two breast cancer, four colorectal cancer and two 
glioblastoma samples. The blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm and mounted onto Xenium 
slides, in accordance with the FFPE Tissue Preparation Guide (10x Genomics, CG000578, Rev 
B). In situ hybridisation was carried out overnight using 380 probes from the pre-designed Xenium 
Immuno Oncology Panel (10x Genomics) and from the add-on custom panel of 76 cuTARs and

components. To annotate cell types for the Xenium cells, label -transfer was performed using 
tissue-matched HN, breast , CRC, GBM publicly available scRNA -seq data with cell type 
annotations (Manuscript 24).
